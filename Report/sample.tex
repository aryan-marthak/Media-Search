\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\thispagestyle{romanplain}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

The proliferation of online product reviews has made them a central factor in
consumer purchase decisions. Simultaneously, the emergence of large language
models has dramatically reduced the cost of producing fluent, natural-sounding
text, enabling the large-scale fabrication of fake and incentivised reviews on
e-commerce platforms. Traditional rule-based filters and single-model
classifiers are no longer sufficient to detect such content reliably.

This report presents \textbf{ReviewIQ}, a production-grade, full-stack
platform for review authenticity analysis built on a microservice
architecture. The system combines three independently fine-tuned transformer
models (a BERT sentiment classifier, a BERT star-rating predictor, and a
RoBERTa AI-generated text detector) with statistical batch analysis
(duplicate detection, rating distribution anomaly analysis, temporal burst
detection, length uniformity scoring, and cross-review phrase repetition) to
produce both per-review authenticity scores and product-level trust reports.

The platform consists of four independently deployable services: a Spring Boot
3.3.5 REST backend (Java~21) providing JWT authentication, role-based access
control, rate limiting via Bucket4j, and AES-256-GCM encryption at rest; a
Python Flask ML inference service exposing the three transformer models
together with KeyBERT keyphrase extraction and a red-flag pattern engine; a
Python Flask web-scraping service built on Playwright and \texttt{httpx}
supporting five major e-commerce platforms across more than ten regional
domains; and a React~19 single-page frontend written in TypeScript with
Tailwind CSS~4 and Recharts. Data is persisted in PostgreSQL~16, with Redis~7
used for rate limiting, token blacklisting, and caching. Reverse proxying,
SSL termination, and static file delivery are handled by Nginx on a
production DigitalOcean droplet. A Chrome extension (Manifest V3) is also
provided for one-click analysis from within supported product pages.

The system has been deployed at \texttt{https://reviewiq.website} and
demonstrates that combining multi-model transformer inference with
statistical corpus-level analysis yields significantly more informative
authenticity signals than any individual approach. This report documents the
problem background, literature context, system analysis, design, detailed
implementation, results, and directions for future work. The complete source
code for the project is publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/MohsinCell/NLP-Review-Authenticity-Analysis}.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background}
Online product reviews have become a foundational trust mechanism for
modern electronic commerce. Research consistently shows that prospective
buyers consult multiple reviews before making purchase decisions, and that
the perceived authenticity of these reviews has a direct and measurable
effect on conversion rates, product rankings, and brand reputation.
However, the economic incentives surrounding reviews have created a
parallel ecosystem of fake, paid, incentivised, and automatically generated
reviews. The recent widespread availability of Large Language Models (LLMs)
such as GPT-style systems has further exacerbated this problem by
dramatically reducing the cost and effort required to produce fluent,
natural-sounding fake reviews at scale.

The scale of the problem is significant. Industry estimates suggest that
between 30 and 40 percent of online reviews on major e-commerce platforms
may be inauthentic, ranging from outright fabricated reviews posted by bots
to subtler forms such as incentivised reviews written by real humans who
received free products or payment in exchange for positive feedback. The
economic consequences are substantial: consumers make purchasing decisions
worth billions of dollars annually based on review content, and a single
fraudulent five-star campaign can meaningfully shift a product's sales
trajectory.

The emergence of generative AI has introduced a qualitative shift in the
threat landscape. Prior to the availability of large language models, fake
reviews could often be detected through surface-level indicators such as
poor grammar, repetitive phrasing, or obviously generic content. Modern
LLMs produce text that is grammatically fluent, contextually appropriate,
and stylistically varied, making traditional detection heuristics largely
ineffective. Furthermore, the marginal cost of generating a fake review
with an LLM is essentially zero, enabling bad actors to produce thousands
of unique, plausible-sounding reviews in minutes.

Conventional detection approaches, such as keyword lists, regular
expression filters, and single-model sentiment classifiers, are
increasingly inadequate against modern machine-generated content. A robust solution
must therefore combine multiple complementary signals: semantic
understanding of the text, cross-checking against expected rating
patterns, explicit detection of machine-authored content, and statistical
analysis of entire review corpora to surface duplicate, coordinated, or
temporally bursty review campaigns.

The Indian e-commerce market presents a particularly interesting case study
for this problem. With platforms like Amazon India, Flipkart, Myntra, Ajio,
and Nykaa serving hundreds of millions of users, the volume of reviews is
enormous and the incentive structures for fake reviews are correspondingly
strong. Many Indian e-commerce platforms also feature reviews in multiple
languages and scripts, adding an additional layer of complexity to any
detection system. ReviewIQ was designed with this multi-platform,
multi-lingual reality in mind from the outset.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given an arbitrary product review (as free text) or a product URL from a
major e-commerce platform, the problem is to determine, to a quantifiable
degree of confidence, whether the review(s) are likely to be authentic
human-authored feedback or whether they exhibit characteristics of fake,
machine-generated, incentivised, or otherwise manipulated content, and to
present these findings through an accessible, interactive interface
suitable for consumers, analysts, and administrators.

More specifically, the problem can be decomposed into several sub-problems:
(a) classifying the sentiment of a review and predicting its expected star
rating to detect mismatches indicative of manipulation; (b) determining
whether a review was generated by an AI system rather than written by a
human; (c) analysing a corpus of reviews for statistical anomalies such as
duplicate content, temporal bursts, and suspiciously uniform review lengths
that suggest coordinated campaigns; and (d) integrating all of these
signals into a single, interpretable authenticity score that end users can
act upon without requiring technical expertise in NLP or statistics.

\section{Objectives}
The primary objectives of the ReviewIQ platform are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item To design and implement a multi-model natural language processing
        pipeline capable of classifying review sentiment, predicting the
        expected star rating, and detecting AI-generated content.
  \item To combine per-review model outputs into a composite
        \textit{authenticity score} (0 to 100) that is intuitive for end
        users while remaining statistically grounded.
  \item To build an automated web-scraping subsystem capable of extracting
        reviews from the leading Indian and international e-commerce
        platforms (Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra, Ajio, Nykaa) while respecting
        \texttt{robots.txt} and applying rate limiting.
  \item To generate corpus-level \textit{trust reports} using statistical
        techniques such as TF-IDF duplicate detection, temporal burst
        analysis, rating distribution anomaly detection, and phrase
        repetition scoring.
  \item To expose the analysis pipeline through a secure, rate-limited
        REST API secured with JWT authentication, role-based access
        control, and AES-256-GCM encryption at rest.
  \item To provide an intuitive single-page web application and a Chrome
        extension for accessing the platform from within e-commerce
        product pages.
  \item To deploy the full system on a production Linux server with
        SSL termination, process management, and log rotation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope of the Project}
The scope of this project covers the end-to-end design, development,
testing, and deployment of a full-stack web platform for review
authenticity analysis. This includes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Training data curation and fine-tuning strategy for three
        transformer models.
  \item Building a Spring Boot 3 backend with modern security practices
        including JWT authentication, role-based access control, rate
        limiting, and AES-256-GCM encryption at rest.
  \item Building Python Flask microservices for ML inference and
        web scraping, with proper process management and logging.
  \item Building a React 19 + TypeScript single page application with
        responsive design, interactive data visualisations, and a modern
        component architecture.
  \item Developing a Manifest V3 Chrome extension for in-browser
        analysis on supported e-commerce product pages.
  \item Production deployment on a DigitalOcean droplet with Nginx,
        systemd, Let's Encrypt SSL, structured logging, and log rotation.
  \item Documentation of the full system architecture, design rationale,
        and implementation details in this report.
\end{itemize}

Explicitly out of scope are: training of transformer architectures
from scratch (pre-trained weights are used as the starting point),
automated large-scale takedown actions on e-commerce platforms,
real-time streaming analytics of review feeds, and mobile application
development (the web interface is responsive and serves mobile users
through the browser).

\section{Motivation}
The motivation for this project arises from a clear and growing gap
between the sophistication of modern fake review generation techniques
and the tools available to ordinary consumers to detect them. While
several commercial services and academic prototypes exist, most are
either closed source, single purpose, limited to English, or restricted
to a single e-commerce platform. ReviewIQ aims to demonstrate that it is
possible to build a production-quality, open, multi-platform, and
multi-lingual review authenticity analysis system using contemporary
open source tools and a disciplined software engineering approach.

A secondary motivation is educational. The process of building ReviewIQ
required integrating knowledge from multiple domains: natural language
processing, transfer learning, web scraping and browser automation,
backend security engineering, frontend development, database design, and
production deployment. The project therefore serves as a practical
demonstration of how these individually well-understood techniques can be
combined into a cohesive, working product. By making the full source code
publicly available, the project also aims to serve as a reference
implementation for students and researchers interested in building similar
multi-service NLP applications.

Additionally, the project was motivated by the observation that most
existing review analysis tools treat each review in isolation. While
per-review analysis is valuable, many forms of review manipulation are
only detectable at the corpus level. A coordinated campaign of individually
plausible reviews can only be identified by examining the temporal
distribution of postings, the similarity between review texts, and the
overall rating distribution. ReviewIQ was specifically designed to combine
both per-review and corpus-level analysis into a unified workflow.

\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Overview}
Research on opinion fraud detection has evolved through three broad
generations: \emph{feature-engineered machine learning}, \emph{deep
learning on text}, and most recently \emph{transformer-based transfer
learning} combined with behavioural signals. This chapter summarises the
key contributions that influenced the design of ReviewIQ and places the
project in the context of the existing body of work. The literature is
organised thematically, progressing from early statistical methods through
deep learning to current transformer-based and hybrid approaches.

\section{Traditional Machine Learning Approaches}
Early work on deceptive review detection treated the problem as a
text-classification task using hand-crafted features such as part-of-speech
frequencies, function word distributions, bag-of-words vectors, TF-IDF
representations, sentiment polarity, and simple readability metrics.
Algorithms such as Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, Support Vector
Machines, and Random Forests achieved reasonable accuracy on curated
datasets, particularly the widely used \textit{Deceptive Opinion Spam}
corpus created by Ott et al.~[3].

The Deceptive Opinion Spam corpus was a landmark contribution because it
provided the first large-scale gold-standard dataset of genuinely
deceptive hotel reviews, obtained through a controlled crowdsourcing
experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk. Using this corpus, Ott et al.~[3]
demonstrated that human judges performed only slightly better than chance
at distinguishing real reviews from fabricated ones, while simple n-gram
classifiers could achieve around 90\% accuracy, establishing that
computational approaches to deception detection were both feasible and
necessary.

Subsequent work by Mukherjee et al.~[4] extended the feature set to
include reviewer-level behavioural signals such as review frequency, rating
deviation from the product mean, and the burstiness of a reviewer's
posting history. These behavioural features proved highly complementary to
textual features, a finding that directly influenced ReviewIQ's decision
to combine per-review NLP analysis with corpus-level statistical signals.

Other notable traditional approaches include Li et al.'s~[7] work on using
positive-unlabelled learning (PU learning) to train classifiers when only
a small number of confirmed fake reviews are available, and Feng et al.'s~[6]
exploration of syntactic tree features (deep syntax) as discriminators for
deceptive text. While these methods achieved respectable performance on
their evaluation datasets, the fundamental limitation of all
feature-engineered approaches is their heavy reliance on surface-level
patterns, which modern language models can easily circumvent by generating
text that mimics the statistical properties of genuine reviews.

\section{Deep Learning on Review Text}
The second generation introduced deep learning models such as CNN, LSTM,
and Bi-LSTM architectures that learned distributed text representations
directly from data. These models improved on manually engineered features
by capturing sequential dependencies and local n-gram patterns, but still
struggled with long context and cross-domain generalisation.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were applied to review text by
treating the text as a one-dimensional signal over word embeddings.
Kim's~[8] influential CNN text classification architecture, which uses
multiple parallel convolutional filters of different widths to capture
n-gram patterns at varying scales, was adapted by several research groups
for fake review detection. The advantage of CNNs is their computational
efficiency and their ability to learn position-invariant local features,
but they struggle to capture long-range dependencies within a review.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and their bidirectional variant
(Bi-LSTM) addressed the long-range dependency limitation by processing
review text sequentially and maintaining a hidden state that can, in
principle, carry information across the entire review length. Several
studies demonstrated that Bi-LSTM models outperformed CNNs on longer
review texts where the deceptive signal was distributed across the full
review rather than concentrated in a single phrase.

A notable hybrid approach combined CNN and LSTM architectures, using
convolutional layers to extract local features and recurrent layers to
model their sequential relationships. While these hybrid models achieved
state-of-the-art results at the time of their publication, they were
ultimately superseded by transformer-based architectures that handle both
local and global context more effectively through the self-attention
mechanism.

The deep learning generation also saw the introduction of word
embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText) as a standard preprocessing step,
replacing the sparse bag-of-words and TF-IDF representations used by
traditional methods. These dense vector representations captured semantic
similarity between words and significantly improved generalisation, but
they were static (context-independent), meaning that a word like ``great''
received the same embedding regardless of whether it appeared in a
genuinely enthusiastic review or in a sarcastic one.

\section{Transformer-Based Approaches}
The release of the BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from
Transformers) architecture by Devlin et al.~[1] shifted the state of
the art for most text classification tasks. BERT introduced two key
innovations that proved transformative for NLP: bidirectional
pre-training using masked language modelling, and a transfer learning
paradigm in which a single pre-trained model could be fine-tuned on a
wide variety of downstream tasks with relatively small labelled datasets.

The core architectural element of BERT is the Transformer encoder, which
uses multi-head self-attention to model relationships between all pairs of
tokens in the input simultaneously. Unlike recurrent models that process
tokens sequentially, the self-attention mechanism allows BERT to capture
long-range dependencies efficiently, making it particularly well-suited
for review text where the authenticity signal may depend on the
relationship between words that are far apart in the text.

Following BERT, several variants were proposed that improved on the
original in various ways. RoBERTa (Robustly Optimised BERT Approach) by
Liu et al.~[2] demonstrated that BERT was significantly undertrained
and that longer training with larger batches, more data, and the removal
of the next-sentence prediction objective yielded substantial improvements
across all benchmarks. DistilBERT achieved comparable performance to BERT
with 40\% fewer parameters through knowledge distillation. ALBERT reduced
memory consumption through parameter sharing across layers. ELECTRA
replaced masked language modelling with a more sample-efficient
replaced-token detection objective.

Several academic works have successfully applied transformer fine-tuning
to fake review detection, sentiment analysis, and aspect-based opinion
mining. Of particular relevance to ReviewIQ is the finding that
task-specific fine-tuning of relatively small transformer models (6 to 12
layers) can match or exceed the performance of much larger models for
well-defined classification tasks, provided sufficient labelled training
data is available. This finding justified ReviewIQ's choice of 6-layer
BERT models for sentiment and rating prediction, which offer a favourable
balance between accuracy and inference speed on CPU hardware.

ReviewIQ builds on this generation by using \emph{three independently
fine-tuned transformers} operating in parallel:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A 6-layer BERT model fine-tuned for ternary sentiment
        classification (positive, negative, neutral). This model was
        trained on a curated dataset of product reviews with sentiment
        labels (evaluation metrics are reported in Chapter~6).
  \item A 6-layer BERT model fine-tuned for 5-class star rating
        prediction (1 through 5 stars). The predicted rating is compared
        against the actual posted rating to detect mismatches indicative
        of manipulation.
  \item A RoBERTa base model fine-tuned for binary human vs.
        AI-generated classification. This model was trained on a balanced
        dataset of human-written and LLM-generated product reviews,
        enabling it to detect the subtle statistical patterns that
        distinguish machine-generated text from human writing.
\end{itemize}

The decision to use three separate specialised models rather than a single
multi-task model was deliberate. While multi-task learning can improve
generalisation in data-scarce settings, the three tasks addressed by
ReviewIQ (sentiment, rating prediction, and AI detection) have
sufficiently different optimal feature representations that independent
fine-tuning yielded better results in practice. Furthermore, independent
models can be updated, retrained, and debugged individually without
affecting the other components of the pipeline.

\section{AI-Generated Text Detection}
With the advent of GPT-style LLMs, a new line of research has emerged
around detecting machine-generated text. This line of work has become
increasingly important as language models have become more accessible
and their output has become harder to distinguish from human writing.

Several distinct approaches to AI-generated text detection have been
proposed in the literature:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Perplexity-based statistical tests.} These methods
        exploit the observation that language models tend to produce text
        with lower perplexity (as measured by the generating model or a
        similar one) than human-written text. The intuition is that
        language models favour high-probability token sequences, while
        human writers make choices that are less statistically predictable.
  \item \textbf{Zero-shot curvature methods.} DetectGPT (Mitchell et al.~[9]) introduced a novel approach based on the observation that
        machine-generated text tends to occupy regions of negative
        curvature in the log-probability landscape of the generating
        model. By applying small perturbations to the text and measuring
        the resulting change in log-probability, DetectGPT can distinguish
        machine-generated from human-written text without any fine-tuning.
  \item \textbf{Watermark-based detectors.} These methods embed
        imperceptible statistical watermarks during the generation process
        that can be detected by a corresponding verification algorithm.
        While effective when the watermark is present, these approaches
        require cooperation from the model provider and can be removed by
        sufficiently determined adversaries through paraphrasing.
  \item \textbf{Supervised transformer classifiers.} These methods
        fine-tune a transformer model (typically BERT or RoBERTa) on a
        labelled dataset of human-written and machine-generated texts.
        The classifier learns to recognise the statistical fingerprint of
        machine generation, including patterns in token frequency,
        sentence structure, and semantic coherence.
\end{itemize}

Supervised fine-tuned detectors currently offer the best
balance of accuracy, speed, and deployment simplicity, and are the
approach adopted for the ReviewIQ AI-generated detector. The RoBERTa
architecture was chosen over BERT for this particular task because
RoBERTa's more robust pre-training procedure produces representations
that are better calibrated for detecting subtle distributional
differences between human and machine text.

\section{Behavioural and Statistical Signals}
Beyond textual content, an important complementary line of work uses
behavioural and structural signals to detect review manipulation at the
corpus level rather than the individual review level. This family of
approaches is motivated by the observation that many forms of review fraud
involve coordinated activity that leaves detectable statistical traces even
when individual reviews appear genuine.

Key behavioural and structural signals explored in the literature include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Reviewer posting velocity.} Genuine reviewers typically
        post reviews at irregular intervals, while fake reviewers (whether
        human or automated) often exhibit unusually high posting rates,
        particularly around product launch dates.
  \item \textbf{Temporal bursts.} A sudden spike in review volume for a
        product, particularly if the reviews are overwhelmingly positive
        or negative, is a strong indicator of a coordinated campaign.
  \item \textbf{Rating distribution skew.} Genuine products tend to have
        a J-shaped or bimodal rating distribution, while products targeted
        by fake review campaigns often show an extreme concentration of
        5-star or 1-star ratings that deviates significantly from natural
        patterns.
  \item \textbf{Duplicate and near-duplicate detection.} Using TF-IDF
        cosine similarity or MinHash-LSH to identify pairs or clusters of
        reviews with suspiciously similar content. Even when individual
        reviews are well-written, the reuse of specific phrases or
        sentence structures across multiple reviews is a reliable
        indicator of coordinated activity.
  \item \textbf{Reviewer graph analysis.} Constructing a bipartite graph
        of reviewers and products and analysing its structure can reveal
        groups of reviewers who consistently review the same products,
        suggesting coordination.
\end{itemize}

These signals are particularly powerful against coordinated
review campaigns that no per-review classifier can reliably flag in
isolation. ReviewIQ incorporates duplicate detection (TF-IDF with a 0.85
cosine similarity threshold), temporal burst detection, rating
distribution anomaly scoring, length uniformity analysis, and
cross-review phrase repetition as part of its trust-report module. The
threshold of 0.85 for cosine similarity was selected through empirical
testing to balance precision (avoiding false positives from reviews that
happen to discuss similar product features) against recall (catching
reviews that are paraphrased copies of a template).

\section{Keyphrase Extraction}
Keyphrase extraction is the task of automatically identifying the most
important and representative phrases from a document. Traditional methods
include TF-IDF based ranking, RAKE (Rapid Automatic Keyword Extraction),
and TextRank, a graph-based algorithm inspired by PageRank. While these
methods are computationally efficient, they operate on statistical
co-occurrence patterns and do not capture semantic meaning.

KeyBERT (Grootendorst~[5]) is a lightweight keyphrase extraction library
that uses sentence-transformer embeddings (default:
\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) to rank candidate n-grams by their cosine
similarity to the document embedding. The approach first generates a dense
vector representation of the full document using a pre-trained sentence
transformer, then generates candidate keyphrases using n-gram extraction,
and finally ranks the candidates by their embedding similarity to the
document embedding. This semantic approach ensures that the extracted
keyphrases are not merely frequent terms but genuinely representative of
the document's meaning.

ReviewIQ uses KeyBERT to surface the most salient terms in each review,
aiding interpretability of the analysis output. When a review receives
a low authenticity score, the extracted keyphrases help users understand
what topics the review addresses and whether the keyphrases are consistent
with genuine product feedback or suggest promotional or templated content.
The \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence transformer model was chosen for
keyphrase extraction because it offers an excellent balance between
embedding quality and inference speed, producing 384-dimensional embeddings
in a fraction of the time required by larger sentence transformers.

\section{Summary of Insights}
The literature converges on three high-level insights that directly shaped
the ReviewIQ design:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No single signal is sufficient.} Combining text-based and
        behavioural signals produces noticeably better discrimination.
  \item \textbf{Transformer fine-tuning is the current sweet spot} for
        per-review text analysis in terms of accuracy, inference cost,
        and deployment complexity.
  \item \textbf{Statistical corpus-level analysis remains indispensable}
        for catching coordinated fake campaigns, even as per-review
        detection improves.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Existing Systems and Their Limitations}
Several commercial and open-source tools attempt to highlight suspicious
reviews. A survey of the most prominent existing systems reveals the
following landscape:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fakespot.} A commercial browser extension and website
        that analyses Amazon product reviews and assigns a letter grade.
        Fakespot uses proprietary algorithms and does not disclose its
        detection methodology. It supports only Amazon and does not
        provide per-review drill-down or corpus-level statistical analysis.
  \item \textbf{ReviewMeta.} A web tool that adjusts Amazon product
        ratings by filtering out reviews it considers untrustworthy based
        on behavioural signals such as unverified purchases, reviewer
        history, and phrase repetition. Like Fakespot, it supports only
        Amazon and does not incorporate AI-generated text detection.
  \item \textbf{GPTZero and similar AI detectors.} General-purpose
        AI-generated text detectors that can be applied to review text.
        These tools focus exclusively on the human vs. machine
        classification task and do not provide any review-specific analysis
        such as sentiment, rating prediction, or corpus-level trust reports.
  \item \textbf{Academic prototypes.} Several research groups have
        published prototype systems for fake review detection, but these
        are typically limited to a single dataset, a single platform, and

\section{Proposed System}
The proposed ReviewIQ platform is an open, full-stack, multi-platform
review authenticity analysis system. It accepts either a single review
text or a product URL; in the latter case it transparently scrapes the
product reviews, submits them through the ML inference pipeline, and
produces a comprehensive product-level trust report. Results are
presented through an interactive dashboard with gauge charts, pie charts,
rating distributions, and per-review drill-down, and the same
functionality is made available through a Chrome extension.

The system is designed around a microservice architecture consisting of
four independently deployable services. The Spring Boot backend serves as
the central orchestrator, handling authentication, authorisation, rate
limiting, encryption, and request routing. The Flask ML service runs the
three transformer models and produces per-review analysis and corpus-level
trust reports. The Flask scraper service handles web scraping across the
five supported platforms using a two-phase extraction strategy with
stealth capabilities. The React frontend provides the user interface for
all interactions, from single-review analysis to full product trust reports
and administrative functions.

The key differentiators of the proposed system compared to existing tools
are: (a) multi-model analysis combining sentiment, rating prediction, and
AI detection rather than relying on a single signal; (b) corpus-level
statistical analysis that can detect coordinated campaigns invisible to
per-review classifiers; (c) support for five major e-commerce platforms
with automated URL-based scraping; (d) automatic language detection and
translation for multi-lingual reviews; (e) full transparency through
open-source availability; and (f) production-grade security including
JWT authentication, rate limiting, and encryption at rest.

\section{Technology Selection Rationale}
The selection of technologies for ReviewIQ was guided by several criteria:
maturity and community support, suitability for the specific task,
performance on constrained hardware, and the developer's prior familiarity
with the ecosystem. The rationale for the major technology choices is as
follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Spring Boot (Java 21) for the backend.} Spring Boot was
        chosen over alternatives such as Django (Python), Express (Node.js),
        and Go because it provides the most mature and battle-tested
        ecosystem for building secure REST APIs. Spring Security, Spring
        Data JPA, and Flyway provide production-grade authentication,
        persistence, and migration support out of the box. Java 21 was
        selected specifically for its virtual threads support (Project
        Loom), modern switch expressions, and record types.
  \item \textbf{Flask (Python) for ML and scraping services.} Python is
        the natural choice for ML workloads due to the PyTorch and
        HuggingFace Transformers ecosystem. Flask was chosen over FastAPI
        because the ML service's API surface is small (five endpoints),
        and Flask's simplicity reduced boilerplate without sacrificing
        functionality. The scraper service uses Flask for the same reason,
        with Playwright providing the browser automation layer.
  \item \textbf{React 19 with TypeScript for the frontend.} React was
        chosen for its component-based architecture, large ecosystem of
        visualisation libraries (Recharts), and strong TypeScript support.
        Vite 7 was selected as the build tool for its fast hot module
        replacement during development and optimised production builds.
        Tailwind CSS 4 was preferred over component libraries like Material
        UI for its utility-first approach that enables rapid UI development
        without fighting framework defaults.
  \item \textbf{PostgreSQL 16 for the database.} PostgreSQL was chosen
        for its reliability, its strong support for JSON columns (useful
        for storing analysis results), and its excellent integration with
        Spring Data JPA and Flyway.
  \item \textbf{Redis 7 for caching and rate limiting.} Redis provides
        the low-latency key-value storage needed for rate-limit token
        buckets and JWT blacklisting. Its atomic operations and TTL
        support make it well-suited for these use cases.
\end{itemize}

\section{Feasibility Study}

\subsection{Technical Feasibility}
All components of the system are built on well-established open-source
technologies with strong community support. Spring Boot, React, PyTorch,
Flask, PostgreSQL, Redis, Nginx, and Playwright are all production-proven
and well documented. Pre-trained transformer weights are freely
available through the HuggingFace ecosystem. Fine-tuning the three
chosen models requires only commodity GPU resources available through
cloud platforms such as Google Colab or Kaggle.

The primary technical risk was whether three transformer models could run
concurrently on a 2~GB RAM server. This risk was mitigated through
several strategies: using 6-layer BERT variants (rather than the full
12-layer models) for sentiment and rating prediction, loading models
sequentially at startup with explicit garbage collection between loads,
and provisioning 2~GB of swap space to handle peak memory during model
warm-up. In practice, the system stabilises at approximately 1.6~GB of
resident memory after all models are loaded, leaving sufficient headroom
for request handling.

\section{Design Goals}
The architectural design of ReviewIQ is guided by the following goals:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Separation of concerns.} Each programming language is
        used for what it is best at: Java for the backend API and security
        layer, Python for ML inference and web scraping, and TypeScript
        for the interactive frontend. This ensures that each service can
        leverage the strongest libraries and idioms of its ecosystem.
  \item \textbf{Independent deployability.} ML models, the scraper, the
        backend, and the frontend can be restarted, updated, or scaled
        independently. A bug fix in the scraper does not require
        redeploying the ML service, and a frontend update does not require
        restarting the backend.
  \item \textbf{Statelessness at the service layer.} All persistent state
        is kept in PostgreSQL or Redis. The backend, ML service, and
        scraper are stateless HTTP services that can be stopped and
        restarted without data loss. This design also enables horizontal
        scaling by simply adding more instances behind a load balancer,
        although the current single-server deployment does not require it.
  \item \textbf{Defence in depth for security.} Security is implemented
        as multiple independent layers: Nginx enforces HTTPS and provides
        basic request filtering; the backend enforces rate limiting via
        Bucket4j, JWT authentication, role-based authorisation, input
        validation via Jakarta Bean Validation, and AES-256-GCM encryption
        at rest. No single layer is solely responsible for security.
  \item \textbf{Observability.} Structured JSON logs, rotating file logs,
        a dedicated security audit log, and Prometheus metrics ensure that
        the system's behaviour can be monitored and debugged in production.
  \item \textbf{Simplicity.} The architecture avoids unnecessary
        complexity. A single Nginx instance handles all reverse proxying
        and SSL termination. Services communicate over plain HTTP on
        localhost. There is no message queue, no container orchestrator,
        and no service mesh, because the current scale does not justify
        their operational overhead.
\end{enumerate}

\section{High-Level Architecture}
The overall architecture is illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}. A single Nginx instance acts as the SSL
termination point and reverse proxy for the entire system. The React
frontend bundle is served as static files by Nginx. Dynamic requests
are routed to the Spring Boot backend (for API calls) or directly to
the scraper service (for scraping endpoints). The backend calls the
Flask ML inference service and the scraper over HTTP, and persists
data in PostgreSQL while using Redis for rate-limit buckets, JWT
blacklists, and short-lived caches.

The communication pattern between services is intentionally simple:
synchronous HTTP requests on localhost. The backend acts as the central
orchestrator for all user-facing operations. When a user submits a
single review for analysis, the backend forwards it to the ML service,
receives the analysis results, optionally encrypts and persists them,
and returns the response to the frontend. When a user submits a product
URL, the backend first calls the scraper service to extract reviews,
then forwards the extracted reviews to the ML service for analysis and
trust report generation, and finally assembles the complete response.

This architecture was chosen over alternatives such as event-driven
messaging (using a message queue like RabbitMQ or Kafka) because the
request/response pattern is a natural fit for the user-facing workflow,
where the user submits a request and waits for results. The additional
complexity and operational overhead of a message queue was not justified
for the current scale of the system.

\section{ML Inference Pipeline Design}
The ML subsystem runs three transformer models in parallel for every
input review. The pipeline consists of three stages: pre-processing,
parallel inference, and score fusion.

\textbf{Pre-processing.} The input review text undergoes several
normalisation steps before being passed to the models. Excessive
whitespace is collapsed, Unicode characters are normalised to NFC form,
and HTML entities (if present from scraping) are decoded. The text is
then truncated to the maximum supported context length of each model
(512 tokens for BERT, 512 tokens for RoBERTa) and tokenised using the
model-specific tokenizer from the HuggingFace Transformers library. If
automatic language detection determines that the review is not in
English, the text is first translated via the LibreTranslate service
before analysis.

\textbf{Parallel inference.} The three models run sequentially within a
single request (true parallelism is limited by CPU resources on the
production server, though the architecture supports parallel execution
on multi-GPU hardware). The sentiment model produces a ternary
classification (positive, negative, neutral) with a confidence score.
The rating model predicts the expected star rating (1 through 5). The
AI detector produces a probability that the text was generated by an
AI system.

\textbf{Score fusion.} After inference, a fusion stage
combines the raw outputs with KeyBERT keywords, red-flag pattern
matches, and language metadata into a composite authenticity score in
the range 0 to 100, which is then returned to the caller. The fusion
algorithm weights the three model outputs according to their relative
importance for authenticity assessment: a high AI probability is the
strongest negative signal, followed by a sentiment-rating mismatch, and
then the presence of red-flag patterns. The weights were tuned
empirically to produce scores that align with human judgement on a
manually labelled validation set.

\section{Scraper Design}
The web scraper employs a two-phase extraction strategy illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:scraper}. The design is motivated by two competing
requirements: speed (users should not wait minutes for results) and
robustness (the scraper must succeed even when platforms deploy
anti-automation measures).

\textbf{Phase 1 (Lightweight Fetch).} The scraper first attempts a
lightweight extraction using \texttt{httpx} against the platform's public
API or mobile HTML surface. This phase is fast (typically completing in
under 5 seconds) and resource-efficient because it does not require a
browser process. For platforms that expose public or semi-public API
endpoints (such as Flipkart's API gateway), Phase 1 can extract all
available reviews directly. For platforms with stricter anti-bot
protections, Phase 1 may succeed partially or fail entirely, triggering
the fallback.

\textbf{Phase 2 (Browser Rendering).} If Phase 1 fails or yields too few
reviews, the scraper falls back to Playwright-driven Chromium with the
full stealth suite installed. Phase~2 applies eleven stealth scripts,
attaches persisted cookies, and parses the rendered DOM. While slower
than Phase 1, this approach can handle JavaScript-rendered review sections,
lazy-loaded content, and platforms that require cookie consent or CAPTCHA
interaction.

All results are then deduplicated via a composite key of review~ID, text
fingerprint (normalised first 150 characters), and rating. The
deduplication step is essential because pagination overlap, lazy loading,
and Phase 1/Phase 2 overlap can all produce duplicate review entries.

\section{Deployment Design}
The production deployment is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:deployment}. All
four services run as \texttt{systemd} units on a single Ubuntu
droplet, behind a single Nginx reverse proxy with Let's Encrypt SSL.

The deployment architecture is designed for reliability on constrained
hardware. Each service runs as a separate \texttt{systemd} unit with
\texttt{Restart=on-failure} and a restart delay, ensuring that transient
failures (such as an out-of-memory condition during peak load) are handled
automatically. The Nginx configuration includes appropriate proxy timeouts,
buffer sizes, and connection limits for each upstream service. Static
frontend assets are served directly by Nginx with aggressive cache headers,
minimising the load on the backend for repeat visits.

The SSL configuration uses Let's Encrypt certificates with automatic
renewal via certbot. The Nginx SSL configuration follows current best
practices, disabling weak ciphers and protocols, enabling OCSP stapling,
and setting appropriate HSTS headers. HTTP requests are redirected to HTTPS
at the Nginx layer, ensuring that all client communication is encrypted.

\subsection{Database Migrations}
Database schema is managed with Flyway, an industry-standard database
migration tool that tracks which migrations have been applied and ensures
that schema changes are applied in order and exactly once. This approach
eliminates the common problem of schema drift between development and
production environments.

Four versioned migrations are included under
\texttt{backend/src/\allowbreak{}main/\allowbreak{}resources/\allowbreak{}db/\allowbreak{}migration/}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \texttt{V1\_\_Initial\_Schema.sql}: Creates the core tables
        (\texttt{users}, \texttt{refresh\_tokens},
        \texttt{review\_analyses}, \texttt{link\_analyses},
        \texttt{analysis\_metrics}, \texttt{site\_visits}) with
        appropriate indices and foreign key constraints.
  \item \texttt{V2\_\_Add\_Contact\_Messages.sql}: Adds the
        \texttt{contact\_messages} table for storing user-submitted
        contact form messages.
  \item \texttt{V3\_\_Add\_Another\_Admin\_User.sql}: Seeds a second
        administrator account for operational redundancy.
  \item \texttt{V4\_\_Update\_Admin\_User.sql}: Updates administrator
        user metadata.
\end{itemize}

Migrations are applied automatically on backend start-up. The Spring Boot
configuration sets \texttt{spring.jpa.hibernate.ddl-auto} to
\texttt{validate}, which means Hibernate verifies that the entity mappings
match the database schema (as managed by Flyway) but does not attempt to
modify the schema itself. This separation of concerns ensures that
Flyway is the single source of truth for schema changes.

\subsection{Security Layer}
Security is a first-class concern in ReviewIQ, implemented as multiple
independent, defence-in-depth layers. The security implementation
comprises the following components:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{JWT access and refresh tokens.} Access tokens are
        short-lived (15 minutes by default) and carry user claims
        including ID, email, and role. They are signed with HMAC-SHA256
        using a server-side secret and validated on every request by the
        \texttt{JwtAuthenticationFilter}. Refresh tokens are long-lived
        (7 days) and persisted (hashed with SHA-256) together with the
        requesting IP and user agent in the \texttt{refresh\_tokens}
        table, enabling per-device revocation. When a user logs out,
        their refresh token is deleted from the database and their access
        token is added to a Redis-backed blacklist for its remaining TTL.

  \item \textbf{BCrypt password hashing} with a work factor appropriate
        for modern hardware. BCrypt is chosen over alternatives like
        SHA-256 or PBKDF2 because it is deliberately slow and resistant
        to GPU-based brute-force attacks. The work factor is configured
        to make each hash computation take approximately 100 milliseconds
        on the production server, balancing security against login latency.

  \item \textbf{Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)} with two roles:
        \texttt{USER} and \texttt{ADMIN}. Admin-only endpoints are
        protected via \texttt{@PreAuthorize("hasRole('ADMIN')")} at the
        controller method level. The Spring Security filter chain is
        configured to permit unauthenticated access to public endpoints
        (health check, login, signup, single review analysis) while
        requiring authentication for all other endpoints.

  \item \textbf{Progressive account lockout} managed by
        \texttt{LoginAttemptService}, which tracks failed login attempts
        per IP address and per email address. After a configurable number
        of consecutive failures, the account is locked for an
        exponentially increasing duration (starting at 1 minute and
        doubling with each subsequent lockout). This mechanism mitigates
        brute-force and credential-stuffing attacks while minimising
        inconvenience to legitimate users who occasionally mistype their
        passwords.

  \item \textbf{SVG CAPTCHA} generated on the frontend using randomised
        text, distortion, and noise, and validated on the backend. The
        CAPTCHA is required on the login and signup forms, mitigating
        automated credential stuffing and account creation bots.

  \item \textbf{Rate limiting} via Bucket4j backed by Redis, with
        distinct token buckets for anonymous users (20 requests per
        minute, keyed by IP address) and authenticated users (60 requests
        per minute, keyed by user ID). Rate limit headers
        (\texttt{X-Rate-Limit-Remaining}, \texttt{X-Rate-Limit-Retry-After})
        are included in responses so that clients can adapt their request
        rate. Exceeded limits return HTTP 429 with a human-readable error
        message.

  \item \textbf{AES-256-GCM encryption at rest} for all stored review
        text and product URLs, implemented in
        \texttt{util.EncryptionUtil}. AES-256-GCM provides both
        confidentiality and integrity (authenticated encryption), ensuring
        that stored data cannot be read or tampered with even if the
        database is compromised. A unique initialisation vector (IV) is
        generated for each encryption operation and prepended to the
        ciphertext. The encryption key is loaded from an environment
        variable at startup and is never written to disk or logged.

  \item \textbf{Email verification via OTP.} New user accounts must
        verify their email address before they can use authenticated
        features. A six-digit one-time password (OTP) is sent to the
        registered email address and must be entered within a configurable
        time window. The OTP is stored as a BCrypt hash in the database,
        preventing exposure even in the event of a data breach.
\end{itemize}

\section{ML Inference Service (Flask + PyTorch)}
The ML service is a single Flask application,
\texttt{ml-service/ml\_service.py}, which dynamically loads three
independently fine-tuned transformer models and the KeyBERT keyphrase
extractor at start-up. Each model lives under \texttt{ml-service/models/}
in its own directory and exposes a prediction function that the main
service loads via \texttt{importlib}.

\subsection{Model Loading}
At start-up, the service changes into each model's directory and
imports its \texttt{predict*.py} module, binding the prediction
function to a global:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,caption={Model loader skeleton (\texttt{ml\_service.py})},label={lst:mlloader}]
def load_models():
    global _sentiment_predictor, _rating_predictor
    global _ai_predictor, _keybert_model, _models_loaded

    if _models_loaded:
        return

    # Sentiment model (6-layer fine-tuned BERT)
    os.chdir(SENTIMENT_DIR)
    sent = _load_module_from_file(
        "predict_sentiment",
        os.path.join(SENTIMENT_DIR, "predict_sentiment.py"))
    _sentiment_predictor = sent.predict_sentiment

    # Rating model (6-layer fine-tuned BERT)
    os.chdir(RATING_DIR)
    rat = _load_module_from_file(
        "predict_rating",
        os.path.join(RATING_DIR, "predict.py"))
    _rating_predictor = rat.predict_review

    # AI-generated detector (fine-tuned RoBERTa)
    os.chdir(AI_GENERATED_DIR)
    ai = _load_module_from_file(
        "predict_ai",
        os.path.join(AI_GENERATED_DIR, "predict.py"))
    _ai_predictor = ai.predict_text

    # KeyBERT using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embeddings
    from keybert import KeyBERT
    _keybert_model = KeyBERT(model='all-MiniLM-L6-v2')

    _models_loaded = True
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Authenticity Score Computation}
After the three model predictions are collected, a fusion function
computes the composite authenticity score. The inputs are the
sentiment label and its confidence, the predicted rating, the AI
probability, and optional language and red-flag information. The
score is reported on a 0 to 100 scale, where higher values indicate
higher authenticity.

The fusion algorithm works as follows:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A base score of 75 is assigned to every review, reflecting
        the assumption that most reviews are genuine until evidence
        suggests otherwise.
  \item The AI probability is the strongest negative signal. If the
        AI detector assigns a probability greater than 0.7, the score is
        reduced by a penalty proportional to the probability, with the
        maximum penalty reaching 40 points for a probability of 1.0.
  \item Sentiment-rating mismatches apply a secondary penalty. If the
        sentiment model classifies the review as strongly positive but
        the rating model predicts 1 or 2 stars (or vice versa), a
        penalty of up to 15 points is applied, scaled by the confidence
        of the sentiment prediction.
  \item The presence of red-flag patterns (promotional language,
        incentivised indicators, excessive superlatives, or suspiciously
        vague content) applies a small additional penalty of up to 10
        points, depending on the number and severity of flags detected.
  \item The final score is clamped to the range 0 to 100 and rounded to
        the nearest integer.
\end{enumerate}

The weighting scheme was tuned empirically by comparing the algorithm's
output against human annotations on a validation set of 200 reviews
spanning all five supported platforms. The goal was to produce scores
that are both discriminative (clearly separating genuine from suspicious
reviews) and calibrated (a score of 80 should correspond to a genuinely
higher likelihood of authenticity than a score of 40).

\subsection{Product Trust Report}
When invoked with a batch of reviews, the ML service produces a trust
report combining several statistical signals. The trust report is
designed to surface patterns that are invisible at the individual review
level but become clear when analysing a corpus as a whole. Each signal
is computed independently and contributes to an overall trust assessment.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Rating distribution anomaly:} The empirical distribution
        of predicted ratings is compared against an expected plausible
        distribution derived from large-scale studies of genuine review
        corpora. Products with an extreme concentration of ratings at a
        single value (particularly 5 stars) are flagged, as are products
        with bimodal distributions that suggest a mix of genuine negative
        feedback and fabricated positive reviews. The anomaly score is
        computed using the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the observed
        and expected distributions.

  \item \textbf{Review burst detection:} Review timestamps are analysed
        to identify statistically significant clusters of postings. The
        algorithm groups reviews into time windows and compares the
        posting rate in each window against the product's baseline rate.
        Windows that exceed the baseline by more than three standard
        deviations are flagged as bursts. Temporal bursts are a strong
        indicator of coordinated campaigns, where multiple fake reviews
        are posted in a short window to boost a product's rating before
        a sale event or to bury negative feedback.

  \item \textbf{Near-duplicate detection:} Reviews are vectorised using
        TF-IDF (term frequency, inverse document frequency) and pairwise
        cosine similarity is computed. Review pairs exceeding a threshold
        of 0.85 are grouped into duplicate clusters and reported in the
        trust report. The TF-IDF representation is computed using unigrams
        and bigrams, with stop words removed and a minimum document
        frequency threshold to eliminate rare terms that can cause
        spurious matches. Duplicate clusters are a reliable indicator of
        template-based fake review campaigns, where a single review
        template is slightly paraphrased and posted multiple times.

  \item \textbf{Length uniformity scoring:} The standard deviation of
        review lengths (in characters) is computed and compared against
        the expected variation for genuine review corpora. A suspiciously
        narrow length distribution (low coefficient of variation) suggests
        that reviews were generated from a template or produced by an
        automated system with a fixed output length parameter.

  \item \textbf{Cross-review phrase repetition:} Frequently occurring
        n-grams (trigrams and 4-grams) that span a disproportionate
        fraction of reviews are identified and reported. While some phrase
        repetition is natural (product names, common adjectives), an
        unusually high repetition rate for specific phrases is indicative
        of template-based or script-generated reviews.

  \item \textbf{Rating-sentiment mismatch rate:} The proportion of
        reviews whose predicted rating disagrees significantly with the
        sentiment label is computed. A high mismatch rate across a product's
        reviews suggests that either the reviews have been manipulated
        (positive text with inflated star ratings) or that the product
        has a genuine disconnect between what reviewers say and how they
        rate it.
\end{itemize}

Each signal is presented in the trust report with both a numerical score
and a human-readable summary, enabling users to understand not just
whether a product's reviews appear suspicious, but specifically what type
of manipulation may be occurring.

\section{Web Scraping Service (Flask + Playwright)}
The scraper is implemented as a Flask application under
\path{web-scraper/review_scraper/web/app.py}. Jobs are orchestrated
by \path{core.scraper_manager.ScraperManager}, which dispatches
each request to one of five platform-specific scrapers:
\texttt{AmazonScraper}, \texttt{FlipkartScraper}, \texttt{MyntraScraper},
\texttt{AjioScraper}, and \texttt{NykaaScraper}.

\subsection{Two-Phase Extraction}
Each scraper attempts a lightweight extraction first using \texttt{httpx}
against the platform's public API or mobile HTML surface. If the
lightweight attempt fails or yields too few reviews, the scraper falls
back to Playwright-driven Chromium with the full stealth suite
installed. This design minimises average latency and resource usage
while retaining the robustness of full browser rendering when needed.

The platform-specific implementations vary significantly in their
Phase 1 strategies:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Amazon.} Phase 1 fetches the mobile HTML review page
        using a mobile user agent and parses the lightweight HTML. This
        works reliably for most Amazon regional domains (India, US, UK,
        Germany, etc.) and typically extracts the first 10 to 20 reviews.
        Phase 2 is used when more reviews are needed or when Amazon
        returns a CAPTCHA challenge.
  \item \textbf{Flipkart.} Phase 1 uses Flipkart's internal API
        endpoints (discovered through reverse engineering of the mobile
        app's network traffic) to fetch reviews in JSON format. This is
        the fastest extraction method across all platforms, often
        completing in under 2 seconds.
  \item \textbf{Myntra.} Phase 1 targets Myntra's review API, which
        returns paginated JSON responses. Phase 2 handles cases where
        the API is rate-limited or returns incomplete data.
  \item \textbf{Ajio.} Phase 1 uses Ajio's product review API. Phase 2
        is triggered when the API response is blocked or when the review
        section requires JavaScript rendering to load.
  \item \textbf{Nykaa.} Phase 1 targets Nykaa's review endpoints for
        both the main Nykaa site and Nykaa Fashion. Phase 2 handles
        JavaScript-heavy review pages that do not expose a clean API.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stealth Suite}
The stealth module ships with eleven scripts covering the most common
automation fingerprints that websites use to detect headless browsers.
Modern e-commerce platforms employ sophisticated bot detection systems
that check dozens of browser properties to distinguish automated access
from genuine human browsing. The ReviewIQ stealth suite addresses these
checks through the following scripts:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{WebDriver flag removal.} Overrides
        \texttt{navigator.webdriver} to return \texttt{false}, preventing
        the most basic automation detection check.
  \item \textbf{WebGL fingerprint spoofing.} Injects noise into WebGL
        rendering output to prevent GPU-based fingerprinting from
        identifying the browser as a headless Chromium instance.
  \item \textbf{Navigator plugin overrides.} Populates
        \texttt{navigator.plugins} with a realistic set of browser plugins
        (PDF Viewer, Chrome PDF Plugin, etc.) that headless browsers
        typically lack.
  \item \textbf{User-agent rotation.} Selects a user agent string from a
        pool of ten realistic desktop and mobile user agents, rotated on
        each new browser context to avoid fingerprint-based session
        tracking.
  \item \textbf{Canvas fingerprint noise.} Applies subtle random noise to
        canvas rendering operations to prevent canvas-based fingerprinting
        from identifying the browser as automated.
  \item \textbf{Language and timezone alignment.} Sets
        \texttt{navigator.language}, \texttt{navigator.languages}, and
        the timezone to values consistent with the selected user agent and
        the target platform's region.
  \item \textbf{Chrome runtime injection.} Adds the
        \texttt{chrome.runtime} object that headless Chromium lacks but
        that detection scripts check for.
  \item \textbf{Permission query override.} Patches the Permissions API
        to return consistent results that match a real browser.
  \item \textbf{Hardware concurrency spoofing.} Sets
        \texttt{navigator.hardwareConcurrency} to a realistic value
        (typically 4 or 8) rather than the default for headless instances.
  \item \textbf{Screen dimensions.} Ensures that \texttt{screen.width},
        \texttt{screen.height}, and \texttt{window.outerWidth/Height}
        return consistent, realistic values.
  \item \textbf{iframe content window patch.} Fixes a known Chromium
        headless detection vector involving cross-origin iframe
        contentWindow access patterns.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cookie Management}
Per-site cookies are persisted in a dedicated cookie store under
\path{web-scraper/cookies/}. A background refresher rotates cookies
on a 30-minute cycle, refreshing proactively before expiry. The
\texttt{/api/cookies/*} endpoints allow operators to import, export,
and inspect cookie freshness per domain.

\subsection{Compliance and Deduplication}
The scraper respects \texttt{robots.txt} before initiating any fetch,
applies configurable rate limiting with randomised delays, and uses
exponential backoff on retries. Extracted reviews are deduplicated by
a composite key composed of the native review ID, a normalised
150-character text fingerprint, and the rating value, eliminating both
duplicates and near-duplicates caused by pagination overlap.

\section{Frontend Implementation (React 19)}
The frontend is a React 19 single-page application written in
TypeScript and built with Vite 7. Styling is provided by Tailwind CSS
4 and animated with Framer Motion. Data visualisation uses Recharts,
including a custom gauge chart for the authenticity score, a
sentiment pie chart, and a rating distribution bar chart.

The frontend was designed with three primary goals: responsiveness
(the interface must work well on desktop, tablet, and mobile screens),
accessibility (interactive elements must be keyboard-navigable and
appropriately labelled), and performance (the initial page load should
be fast and subsequent navigation should feel instantaneous through
client-side routing).

Vite 7 was chosen as the build tool because it provides near-instant
hot module replacement during development (thanks to its use of native
ES modules) and highly optimised production builds with tree shaking,
code splitting, and minification. The production build generates
separate chunks for vendor libraries and application code, enabling
efficient browser caching of third-party dependencies that change
infrequently.

Tailwind CSS 4 provides utility-first styling, which allows rapid UI
development without writing custom CSS classes. The utility-first
approach also naturally prevents specificity conflicts and ensures
consistent spacing, typography, and colour usage across the application.
Responsive breakpoints are handled through Tailwind's built-in responsive
prefixes (\texttt{sm:}, \texttt{md:}, \texttt{lg:}), ensuring that the
same components render appropriately across all screen sizes.

Framer Motion provides declarative animation primitives that enable
smooth page transitions, element entry/exit animations, and interactive
hover and tap effects. Animations are used judiciously throughout the
application to provide visual feedback (loading states, success
confirmations) without becoming distracting.

\subsection{Directory Structure}
Source code is organised under \texttt{frontend/src/}:

\subsection{State and Routing}
Client-side routing is handled by React Router 7, which maps URL paths
to page components and handles navigation without full page reloads. The
routing configuration defines both public routes (accessible without
authentication) and protected routes (requiring a valid JWT). Protected
routes are wrapped in a \texttt{ProtectedRoute} component that checks
the authentication state and redirects to the login page if the user is
not authenticated.

Authentication state is managed in an \texttt{AuthContext} that stores
the access token in memory (not in local storage, to reduce XSS risk),
refreshes tokens transparently on 401 responses via an Axios interceptor,
and exposes hooks for guarded routes. The Axios interceptor is configured
to retry a failed request exactly once after refreshing the access token,
preventing infinite refresh loops in the event that the refresh token
itself has expired.

The frontend communicates with the backend through a centralised
\texttt{services/} layer that abstracts all API calls into typed
functions. Each service module corresponds to a backend controller
(e.g. \texttt{authService.ts} for authentication, \texttt{reviewService.ts}
for analysis endpoints). This separation ensures that API URL changes or
response format updates require modifications in only one place rather
than throughout the component tree.

\section{Chrome Extension}
The Chrome extension (\texttt{chrome-extension/manifest.json}) uses
Manifest~V3, the latest extension API specification required by Google
Chrome. The extension provides a convenient one-click analysis workflow
for users browsing products on supported e-commerce platforms, eliminating
the need to copy and paste product URLs into the ReviewIQ website.

The extension declares the following permissions and configuration:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Permissions:} \texttt{activeTab} (to read the current
        tab's URL) and \texttt{storage} (to persist user preferences and
        authentication tokens).
  \item \textbf{Host permissions:}
        \texttt{https://reviewiq.website/*} for communicating with the
        ReviewIQ backend API.
  \item \textbf{Content scripts:} Matched against all supported
        e-commerce domains, including ten Amazon regional domains
        (\texttt{amazon.in}, \texttt{amazon.com}, \texttt{amazon.co.uk},
        \texttt{amazon.de}, etc.), Flipkart, Myntra, Ajio, Nykaa, and
        Nykaa Fashion.
\end{itemize}

When the user clicks the browser action icon on a supported product page,
the extension popup extracts the current tab's URL, validates that it
matches a supported platform and product page pattern, and sends an
analysis request to the ReviewIQ backend. The popup displays a progress
indicator during scraping and analysis, and upon completion, shows a
summary of the results with a link to the full interactive report on the
ReviewIQ website.

The extension uses \texttt{chrome.storage.local} to persist the user's
JWT access and refresh tokens, enabling authenticated analysis requests
that are recorded in the user's history. If the user is not logged in,
the extension prompts them to log in through the ReviewIQ website before
initiating an analysis.

\section{Deployment}
Production deployment is automated through shell scripts under the
\texttt{deploy/} directory. The deployment automation was designed to
enable reproducible, single-command provisioning of a fresh Ubuntu
server from bare metal to a fully operational ReviewIQ instance.

The \texttt{deploy.sh} script performs the following steps in order:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{System update and dependency installation.} Updates the
        package index and installs Java~21 (via the Adoptium APT
        repository), Node.js (via the NodeSource repository), Python 3.10+,
        PostgreSQL~16, Redis~7, Nginx, and certbot.
  \item \textbf{Firewall configuration.} Configures \texttt{ufw} to
        allow SSH (port 22), HTTP (port 80), and HTTPS (port 443), and
        denies all other inbound traffic. Internal service ports (5000,
        5001, 8080) are accessible only from localhost.
  \item \textbf{Swap provisioning.} Creates a 2~GB swap file to
        supplement the 2~GB physical RAM, ensuring that the system can
        handle peak memory usage during transformer model loading
        without triggering the OOM killer.
  \item \textbf{Database setup.} Creates the PostgreSQL user and
        database, and sets appropriate permissions.
  \item \textbf{Frontend build.} Installs npm dependencies and builds
        the React application in production mode, producing optimised
        static assets in the \texttt{dist/} directory.
  \item \textbf{Backend build.} Builds the Spring Boot application using
        Maven, producing an executable JAR file.
  \item \textbf{Python service installation.} Creates Python virtual
        environments for the ML service and scraper, installs their
        dependencies, and installs Playwright's Chromium browser.
  \item \textbf{SSL certificate provisioning.} Obtains Let's Encrypt
        certificates using certbot's standalone mode, then configures
        Nginx for SSL termination with the obtained certificates.
  \item \textbf{Nginx configuration.} Writes the Nginx configuration
        with appropriate location blocks for the frontend (static files),
        backend API (\texttt{/api/*}), ML service, and scraper service.
  \item \textbf{Systemd unit creation.} Creates and enables
        \texttt{systemd} service units for the backend
        (\texttt{reviewiq-backend.service}), ML service
        (\texttt{reviewiq-ml.service}), and scraper
        (\texttt{reviewiq-scraper.service}), each configured with
        appropriate environment variables, restart policies, and resource
        limits.
  \item \textbf{Log rotation configuration.} Configures \texttt{logrotate}
        rules for application logs (50~MB rotation, 30-day retention,
        1~GB cap) and security audit logs (90-day retention).
  \item \textbf{Service startup.} Starts all services and verifies
        that they are healthy by polling their health check endpoints.
\end{enumerate}

The entire deployment process, from a fresh Ubuntu server to a fully
operational ReviewIQ instance with SSL, takes approximately 15 to 20
minutes, depending on network speed for downloading dependencies and
pre-trained model weights.

\section{Performance Observations}
Empirical observations on the production droplet indicate that a
single-review analysis request typically completes well within the
two-second target, dominated by transformer inference on CPU. The
breakdown of a typical single-review analysis is approximately:
100 to 200 milliseconds for pre-processing and language detection,
400 to 600 milliseconds for sentiment model inference, 400 to 600
milliseconds for rating model inference, 300 to 500 milliseconds for
the AI detection model, and 100 to 200 milliseconds for KeyBERT
keyphrase extraction, red-flag detection, and score fusion. The total
wall-clock time is typically 1.2 to 1.8 seconds, well within the
two-second target.

Product URL analysis is inherently more variable, because scraping
latency depends heavily on the target platform, the number of reviews
available, and whether Phase~2 Playwright rendering is required.
Phase 1 scraping typically completes in 2 to 5 seconds, while Phase 2
may take 10 to 30 seconds depending on page load time and the number of
review pages to paginate through. The ML analysis of the scraped reviews
adds an additional 1 to 2 seconds per review. The system streams status
updates to the frontend so that the user always sees live progress,
including the number of reviews scraped and the current analysis stage.

Memory usage on the production server stabilises at approximately 1.6~GB
of resident memory after all transformer models are loaded, with the
remaining 400~MB of physical RAM available for request handling,
PostgreSQL, Redis, and Nginx. The 2~GB swap partition provides an
additional safety margin for occasional memory spikes during concurrent
requests.

The sentiment model achieves 91.2\% accuracy on a balanced test split of

subjective boundary between neighbouring star ratings. When the
tolerance is relaxed to $\pm 1$ star, accuracy rises to 96.3\%.

The AI-generated text detector, based on the publicly available
\texttt{roberta-base-openai-detector} model, achieves 95.1\% accuracy
on a mixed corpus of human-written product reviews and LLM-generated
reviews (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 outputs). Performance degrades on heavily
paraphrased or short texts (under 50 tokens), which is a known
limitation of all current AI detectors.

\section{Security Verification}
The security posture of the platform was verified through the
following spot-checks:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Rate limits fire on both anonymous and authenticated buckets
        under burst testing.
  \item Access to admin endpoints is rejected for users with the
        \texttt{USER} role.
  \item Invalid or expired JWTs are rejected at the filter layer.
  \item Database rows for \texttt{review\_analyses} and
        \texttt{link\_analyses} are stored as ciphertext; the plaintext
        never leaves the application layer.
  \item BCrypt password hashes are used, with the work factor
        calibrated for modern hardware.
  \item HTTPS is enforced at the Nginx layer; HTTP requests are
        redirected.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
The combination of transformer models, heuristic red-flag detection,
and corpus-level statistical analysis produced noticeably more
informative authenticity signals than any single technique on its own.
In particular, the trust report was effective at surfacing coordinated
review campaigns that individual per-review classifiers would have
missed, while transformer inference captured subtle machine-generated
content that rule-based filters would not have flagged.

Several observations from the development and testing process are worth
highlighting:

\textbf{Multi-model analysis adds genuine value.} During testing, several
cases were identified where one model alone would have produced a
misleading result, but the combination of models provided a more accurate
assessment. For example, a review with positive sentiment and a high
authenticity score from the sentiment model was correctly flagged by the
AI detector as likely machine-generated, resulting in a lower overall
authenticity score. Conversely, genuinely negative reviews that might have
been flagged as suspicious by a simple sentiment-only approach received
appropriate authenticity scores because the rating predictor confirmed the
negative sentiment was consistent with the review content.

\textbf{Corpus-level analysis catches what per-review analysis misses.}
In product URL analysis testing, several products were identified where
individual reviews appeared genuine (moderate-to-high per-review
authenticity scores) but the trust report revealed suspicious patterns:
clusters of near-duplicate reviews, temporal bursts of positive reviews
shortly before sale events, and rating distributions with anomalous
5-star concentration. These findings validated the literature's
observation that no single per-review classifier is sufficient for
comprehensive fake review detection.

\textbf{The microservice architecture proved well-suited to the problem.}
Spring Boot provided mature security primitives and persistence, while
Python provided the ML and scraping tooling. The clean HTTP boundary
between services made it straightforward to develop, test, and debug each
service independently, and the independent deployability meant that ML
model updates could be deployed without restarting the backend or
vice versa.

\textbf{Scraper robustness remains a continuous challenge.} E-commerce
platforms regularly update their front-end code, API endpoints, and
anti-bot measures, requiring corresponding updates to the scraper. The
two-phase design and stealth suite mitigate this to a significant degree,
but ongoing maintenance is an inherent cost of any web scraping system.
The cookie management system, which allows administrators to import
fresh cookies through the admin dashboard, has proven valuable for
handling platforms that require session cookies for access.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Scope}

\section{Conclusion}
This project set out to build a production-quality platform for
detecting fake, AI-generated, and otherwise inauthentic product reviews
across major e-commerce platforms. The resulting ReviewIQ system
delivers on that goal as a full-stack, multi-service application. It
combines three independently fine-tuned transformer models, KeyBERT
keyphrase extraction, a heuristic red-flag engine, and statistical
corpus-level analysis into a single authenticity pipeline, exposed
through a secure REST API, a modern React frontend, and a Chrome
extension. The system has been deployed in production on a
DigitalOcean droplet with SSL, monitoring, and log rotation.

The key technical contributions of the project are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A multi-model NLP pipeline} that combines sentiment
        analysis, star rating prediction, and AI-generated text detection
        into a composite authenticity score that is more informative than
        any individual signal.
  \item \textbf{A corpus-level trust report engine} that uses TF-IDF
        duplicate detection, temporal burst analysis, rating distribution
        anomaly scoring, length uniformity analysis, and phrase repetition
        detection to surface coordinated review manipulation campaigns.
  \item \textbf{A multi-platform web scraping system} supporting five
        major Indian and international e-commerce platforms through a
        two-phase extraction strategy with comprehensive browser stealth.
  \item \textbf{A production-grade security implementation} with JWT
        authentication, role-based access control, progressive account
        lockout, token-bucket rate limiting, and AES-256-GCM encryption
        at rest.
  \item \textbf{A fully automated deployment pipeline} that provisions
        a fresh server and brings the entire platform online with a single
        script invocation.
\end{enumerate}

The project demonstrates how modern open-source tooling (Spring Boot
on the backend, PyTorch and HuggingFace Transformers on the ML layer,
Playwright for resilient web scraping, and React with Tailwind on the
frontend) can be assembled into a coherent, maintainable,
production-grade application by a single engineer, while adhering to
established practices in security, observability, and deployment
automation. The system handles the full lifecycle from raw product URL
to interactive trust report, integrating web scraping, NLP inference,
statistical analysis, and data visualisation in a seamless workflow.

The full source code, model training scripts, and deployment
configuration are maintained in the project repository at
\url{https://github.com/MohsinCell/NLP-Review-Authenticity-Analysis}.

\section{Limitations}
The current implementation has several known limitations that should be
acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{CPU-based inference.} Transformer inference on CPU is
        the primary performance bottleneck. While single-review analysis
        completes within the two-second target, batch analysis of large
        product review corpora (50+ reviews) can take several minutes.
        GPU-based inference would reduce this by an order of magnitude
        but requires more expensive hosting infrastructure.
  \item \textbf{Platform coverage.} The five supported e-commerce
        platforms (Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra, Ajio, Nykaa) cover a large
        share of the Indian and international markets but are by no means
        exhaustive. Adding support for additional platforms requires
        implementing a new platform-specific scraper, which involves
        reverse engineering the target platform's HTML structure or API
        endpoints.
  \item \textbf{Adversarial robustness.} The AI-generated text detector,
        like all such models, can be defeated by sufficiently careful
        paraphrasing of LLM output. As language models improve, the
        distinction between human and machine text becomes increasingly
        subtle, requiring periodic retraining of the detector on fresh
        data.
  \item \textbf{Language coverage.} The system's language coverage is
        best for English. Non-English reviews are handled via translation
        through LibreTranslate, which introduces both additional latency
        (typically 200 to 500 milliseconds per review) and a potential
        accuracy trade-off, as translation may alter subtle linguistic
        cues that the models rely on for authenticity assessment.
  \item \textbf{Scraper maintenance.} The scraper relies on cookies and
        stealth techniques that must be maintained over time as target
        sites evolve their anti-bot measures. While the two-phase design
        and stealth suite provide significant resilience, periodic updates
        are necessary to maintain scraping reliability.
  \item \textbf{Single-server deployment.} The current deployment runs all
        services on a single 2~GB RAM server, which limits concurrent
        request capacity. Under heavy load, the system may experience
        increased latency or occasional out-of-memory conditions during
        peak transformer inference.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Scope}
Several directions are worth exploring in follow-up work. These are
listed in approximate order of expected impact:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{GPU-backed inference.} Deploying the ML service to a
        GPU-equipped instance (or using ONNX Runtime with quantisation)
        would increase throughput for large product scrapes by an order of
        magnitude. ONNX export with INT8 quantisation would also reduce
        model size and memory footprint, potentially enabling the entire
        system to run on a smaller server.

  \item \textbf{Multi-lingual fine-tuning.} Fine-tuning transformer
        models on multi-lingual data (e.g.\ XLM-RoBERTa, which supports
        over 100 languages) would remove the current dependency on
        translation for non-English reviews. This is particularly
        important for the Indian market, where reviews are frequently
        written in Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, and other languages.
        Direct multi-lingual inference would be both faster and more
        accurate than the current translate-then-analyse pipeline.

  \item \textbf{Reviewer-graph analysis.} Extending the trust report to
        include reviewer graph features would significantly improve
        detection of coordinated fraud. By constructing a bipartite graph
        of reviewers and products, the system could identify suspicious
        patterns such as groups of reviewers who consistently review the
        same set of products, reviewers with abnormally high posting
        rates, and reviewers whose account creation dates cluster
        suspiciously close to campaign start dates.

  \item \textbf{Additional platforms.} Adding support for more
        e-commerce platforms (e.g.\ Meesho, Tata CLiQ, Shein, Walmart,
        eBay) would broaden the system's usefulness. The modular scraper
        architecture makes it straightforward to add new platform
        implementations without modifying existing code.

  \item \textbf{Streaming and webhook API.} A server-sent events (SSE)
        or WebSocket streaming API would improve the user experience of
        long-running scrapes by pushing real-time progress updates and
        partial results to the frontend. A webhook API would enable
        integration with external monitoring systems, Slack channels, or
        email alerts for automated periodic analysis of tracked products.

  \item \textbf{Model explainability.} Integrating attention
        visualisations or SHAP-based (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
        feature attribution would make the authenticity score easier to
        trust and interpret. Users could see which specific words or
        phrases in a review contributed most to its authenticity score,
        providing transparency into the model's decision-making process.

  \item \textbf{Automated model retraining pipeline.} A scheduled
        pipeline that re-fine-tunes the models on freshly labelled data
        would help the system keep pace with evolving fake-review
        generation techniques. This pipeline could incorporate active
        learning, where reviews that the current model is most uncertain
        about are prioritised for human labelling.

  \item \textbf{Browser extension for Firefox and Edge.} Porting the
        Chrome extension to the Firefox and Edge WebExtension APIs
        would reach additional users. The Manifest V3 extension format
        is largely compatible across browsers, so the porting effort
        would primarily involve adjusting browser-specific API calls.

  \item \textbf{Mobile application.} While the responsive web interface
        serves mobile users adequately, a native mobile application
        (built with React Native to share code with the web frontend)
        could provide a smoother experience, push notifications for
        completed analyses, and camera-based barcode scanning for quick
        product lookups.

  \item \textbf{Comparative product analysis.} A feature that allows
        users to compare the review authenticity of competing products
        side-by-side would be valuable for purchase decisions. This would
        involve running trust reports for multiple products and presenting
        the results in a comparative dashboard.
\end{enumerate}
